Executive Summary & Ethical Findings

Four cases from Yao et al. (2024) examined through the Problem of Many Hands.

Download Report (PDF)

Central Thesis: Secure & Attractive ≠ Responsible

Yao et al. (2024) address two barriers to smart meter data sharing—privacy/security (via technical architectures) and willingness (via financial rewards)—while leaving moral responsibility unexamined. Applying the Problem of Many Hands (van de Poel & Royakkers, 2011), each actor in the data pipeline performs a small, legal, and technically secure step (“My part was secure and legal”), leaving systemic household harms without an accountable owner.

Case 01 • Searchable Encryption

1. Secure for Whom?

Retailers upload encrypted 15-minute readings so the cloud sees only ciphertext. Yet once a buyer purchases a search token and key, they decrypt plain 15-minute load profiles revealing waking, cooking, and occupancy—with no binding purpose limits after decryption.

Case 02 • City Platform

2. One Platform, Many Hands

A municipal Center of Energy Big Data packages energy data into a “Product Supermarket” relying on a security block and GDPR. Because privacy is collective (Regan, 2015), neighboring households still expose opted-out citizens and create panoptic self-censorship.

Case 03 • Edge & Federated Learning

3. Data Stay Home, Inferences Travel

Federated split learning keeps raw 15-minute data on the end meter while edge and cloud servers aggregate models. However, shared models, forecasts, and automated demand-response signals still travel outward and steer household daily routines without clear ownership of model uses.

Case 04 • HiFi-Gas Incentives

4. Who Gets Paid, Who Is Asked?

Gas companies and heating stations are scored on data quality and rewarded for improving federated forecasting (81.1% → 93.0%). Meanwhile, households—whose consumption generates the data—are neither asked for informed consent (BEIS, 2018: willing ≠ informed) nor paid.

Takeaway: Responsible smart-grid data governance requires a named hand at every step—binding purpose limits, democratic legitimacy, accountability for model inferences, and household inclusion.
